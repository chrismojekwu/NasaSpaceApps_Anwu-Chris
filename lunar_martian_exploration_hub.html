THE MOON // LUNA

Earth’s sole natural satellite—a tidally locked, airless world preserving 4.5 billion years of solar system impact history and polar water-ice reserves.

Surface Landmark Beacon

Sea of Tranquility (Mare Tranquillitatis)

COORDINATES: 0.6741° N, 23.4730° E • ELEVATION: -1.8 KM

A vast basaltic lunar mare formed by ancient volcanic eruptions over 3.5 billion years ago. Famous as the historic landing site of Apollo 11 on July 20, 1969, where humans first walked on another celestial body.

Avg Distance 384,400 km
Surface Gravity 1.62 m/s² (0.166g)
Surface Temp -173° to 127°C
Solar Day 29.5 Earth Days
Atmosphere Ultra-thin Exosphere (Helium, Neon, Argon, Hydrogen)

Earth Locations to simulate the Moon

Extreme Cold

Antartica

These polar regions experience prolonged periods of darkness and very low-angle sunlight, mirroring the lighting challenges at the lunar South Pole. Concordia Station also offers extreme isolation and sub-zero temperatures to test crew psychology and cold-weather hardware.

Extreme Cold

Devon Island, Canada

Located in the High Arctic, this barren polar desert features permafrost, freezing temperatures, and rugged terrain used extensively to test lunar rovers and spacesuits.

Volcanic Terrain

Craters of the Moon National Monument

This vast volcanic landscape features lava flows, cinder cones, and fine basaltic dust that closely resemble the geology and abrasive nature of lunar regolith.

Volcanic Terrain

Mount Etna

The European Space Agency (ESA) uses these volcanic fields to train astronauts in geological sampling, autonomous rover navigation, and operating in dark, jagged basaltic environments.

Volcanic Terrain

Mauna Kea, Hawaii

The high-altitude volcanic slopes provide fine, dusty basaltic soil (used to make lunar regolith simulants) and low-humidity conditions ideal for testing resource extraction and construction tech.

Subsurface

Caves of Lanzarote

These underground volcanic lava tubes serve as testbeds for future lunar habitats, helping engineers study how subsurface structures can shield astronauts from radiation and extreme temperature swings.

Celestial Body 02 // Terrestrial Planet

MARS // THE RED PLANET

A cold, iron-oxide desert world home to the solar system’s tallest volcano, deepest canyon, ancient river deltas, and humanity’s next great frontier.

Surface Landmark Beacon

Olympus Mons

COORDINATES: 18.65° N, 226.2° E • SUMMIT HEIGHT: 21.9 KM

The largest known shield volcano in the solar system—nearly three times the height of Mount Everest and spanning 600 km across, sustained by Mars’ stationary crust over a persistent magma hotspot.

Avg Distance 225M km
Surface Gravity 3.72 m/s² (0.38g)
Surface Temp -140° to 20°C
Martian Sol 24h 39m 35s
Atmosphere 95.3% CO₂ (2.6% N₂, 1.9% Ar • 0.6% Earth pressure)

Earth Locations to simulate Mars

Hyper-Arid Deserts

Atacama Desert, Chile

As the driest non-polar desert on Earth, the Atacama mimics Mars's extreme lack of moisture, high ultraviolet radiation, and salty, nutrient-poor soil. NASA uses this site to test life-detection instruments and autonomous rovers.

Hyper-Arid Deserts

Wadi Rum, Jordan

Known as the Valley of the Moon, this desert features towering sandstone mountains and iron-rich red sand that mirror the visual landscape and dusty conditions of the Martian surface.

Hyper-Arid Deserts

Hanksville, Utah

Operated by The Mars Society, this remote desert region contains iron-rich clay and geological formations resembling ancient Martian riverbeds. Crews live in a simulated habitat here to test field science protocols and teamwork.

Extreme Cold

McMurdo Dry Valleys

These ice-free polar valleys represent the closest terrestrial match to the cold, hyper-arid conditions of Mars. Scientists study microbial life in the frozen soil to understand how organisms might survive on the Red Planet.

Extreme Cold

Devon Island, Canada

Located in the High Arctic, this uninhabited island features a massive impact crater and freezing polar desert conditions. It serves as a primary site for testing spacesuits, drills, and habitats in sub-zero isolation.

Volcanic Terrain

Mauna Loa, Hawaii

Situated at 8,200 feet on a barren volcanic slope, this solar-powered habitat isolates crews for months at a time. The surrounding basaltic lava flows mimic the volcanic geology of Mars, while the isolation tests psychological resilience and delayed communications.

Volcanic Terrain

Rio Tinto, Spain

This river basin contains high concentrations of iron and sulfur, creating acidic, rust-colored water and rock formations. Researchers study the area to understand the mineralogical history of Mars and test subsurface drilling equipment.
